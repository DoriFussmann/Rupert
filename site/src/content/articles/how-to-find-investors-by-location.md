## Why Location Is a Filter, Not a Strategy

Knowing that you want to find investors near you is a reasonable starting point. Knowing *how* to pull a disciplined, geo-filtered list from the tools that actually hold that data — and knowing how to avoid the thousands of irrelevant results those tools also return — is the work that separates founders who close rounds from founders who spend three months emailing the wrong people.

This guide walks through a five-step process for building a workable, location-based investor list: choosing the right database for your investor type, setting filters correctly, validating each name before outreach, supplementing databases with sources that databases miss, and organising everything into a prioritised first-contact plan. Each step is practical and executable. None of them require a $20,000 institutional subscription.

---

## Step 1: Choose the Right Database for Your Investor Type

Not all investor databases cover the same ground, and using the wrong tool for your stage is one of the most common ways founders waste time on location searches. The four platforms founders encounter most — AngelList/Wellfound, Crunchbase, OpenVC, and PitchBook — differ meaningfully in their coverage depth, price, and the type of investor they actually index.

AngelList signals best for pre-seed and seed, while PitchBook covers active check writers from Series A upward.
 That distinction matters the moment you apply a location filter. A pre-seed founder searching for angels in Austin on PitchBook will find relatively few results, because most of those angels simply aren't in the database. Run the same search on AngelList or a founder-oriented tool like OpenVC and the picture changes.

OpenVC is a free, community-sourced database with over 10,000 verified profiles; founders can browse curated lists and filter by industry, stage, and geography.
 It's a strong starting point for early-stage founders who need geographic coverage without a subscription. 
Crunchbase focuses on company and funding data rather than verified investor contact details, making it best suited to founders who want to identify potential investors and then do their own outreach through LinkedIn or email research.

Crunchbase Pro starts around $29 per month, while institutional tools like PitchBook run upwards of $20,000 per year and are built for VC firms and analysts, not bootstrapped founders.
 For most early-stage founders working a location-based search, an affordable subscription database that offers verified contacts and export tools gives the best balance of coverage and cost. 
The best investor database is the one that fits your stage, budget, and outreach style — free directories work for early research, institutional tools like PitchBook fit VC firms, and founder-focused databases offer the best balance for startups actively raising.

One practical approach: use OpenVC or AngelList for the initial discovery pass because they're either free or low-cost, then cross-reference names in Crunchbase to see portfolio history and deal dates before you reach out.

## Step 2: Set Your Filters Correctly — Location Is Just the Starting Point

This is where most founders go wrong. Filtering by location alone — even filtering by a specific city — returns thousands of investors with no meaningful connection to your stage, sector, or check size. The list is too large to work with and too noisy to personalise from. The goal of database filtering is not to find everyone in a geography; it is to find the 30 to 80 investors in that geography who are genuinely plausible fits.

Layering filters is how you get there. Start with your geographic unit: a specific city, a metro area, or a state, depending on how densely the investor community is distributed in your target region. Then add stage — pre-seed, seed, or early-stage Series A, whichever matches your current raise. Then layer in check size range if the database exposes it, which cuts out funds that are too large to care about your round or too small to write a meaningful cheque. Finally, add sector or thesis tags — 
filtering by thesis keywords cuts irrelevant outreach by more than half, and that work should happen before you write a single email.

Each investor profile typically includes investment focus, past investments, company affiliations, and contact details — and the purpose is to help founders find and reach the right investors quickly by filtering by stage, industry, and location to build a targeted list of investors who are actively funding startups like theirs.
 When your filters are properly layered, the database is doing qualification work that would otherwise take hours of manual research per name.

A practical target: after applying all filters, aim for a raw list of 60 to 120 names. You'll lose roughly half during validation (Step 3), leaving you with a clean, prioritised shortlist of 30 to 80 — a number large enough to run a real process, small enough to personalise meaningfully.

## Step 3: Validate Every Investor Before You Write a Word

A database entry is a data point, not a green light. Before any name makes it onto your outreach list, three validation checks should pass.

**Check activity recency first.** 
Portfolio data older than 18 months often points to a fund in harvest mode rather than active deployment — screen for it before you pitch.
 An investor who made their last known investment in 2023 may be raising their next fund, may have shifted thesis, or may simply have stopped writing checks at your stage. None of those situations justify spending time on personalised outreach.

**Check the portfolio for direct competitors second.** Most investors won't back two direct competitors from the same fund cycle. A 60-second scan of their portfolio page on Crunchbase or their firm's website will tell you whether they already have a position in your category. If they do, remove the name — not because the investor is bad, but because that specific conversation has a structural ceiling.

**Look for current thesis signals third.** 
A useful way to judge any database is to ask whether you can see recency of investments, filter by stage, narrow by geography, match by sector thesis, and trust the contact information enough to act on it — if a platform cannot answer those questions, it is a list, not a workflow.
 The same logic applies to individual investor research: their most recent writing, LinkedIn posts, conference appearances, or public comments about what they're funding right now is far more valuable than their database profile, which may be months out of date.

Validation turns a raw database export into a qualified list. It also means every personalised message you write is grounded in genuine research rather than database boilerplate.

## Step 4: Supplement Databases with Sources That Databases Miss

Paid and free databases have a shared blind spot: private investors who don't publicise their activity. Angels who write cheques informally, local high-net-worth individuals who back companies in their city, and family offices that invest selectively but never announce rounds — none of these show up reliably in any commercial database, regardless of what filters you apply. Four non-database sources consistently surface these investors.

**Local accelerator alumni lists.** Accelerators in your city often publish or share lists of their demo day investors — the angels and syndicate leads who attend those events are, by definition, interested in local companies at early stages. A quick message to the accelerator's community manager asking who typically invests on demo day is often enough.

**State innovation office directories.** Many US states operate economic development offices that maintain lists of accredited investors, angel networks, and regional venture funds as part of their mandate to attract capital. These directories are public, free, and routinely overlooked.

**Regional business journals' investor lists.** Most major metro areas have a business journal that publishes annual lists of top local investors, most active angels, or notable venture activity. These lists are curated by editors with strong local knowledge, not scraped from SEC filings, which means they often surface investors who actively want visibility in their region.

**LinkedIn searches for 'angel investor [city]'.** 
Platforms like OpenVC, Gust, and AngelList do not provide direct email access to investors — they rely on in-platform messaging or application submissions
 — but LinkedIn searches for angel investor titles in your target city frequently return profiles of investors who are never indexed in paid databases. Search 'angel investor [city]', 'venture partner [metro]', or 'startup investor [region]' and sort by connections to identify people with warm intro paths already in your network.

The investors surfaced through these non-database sources often have the highest conversion rates, precisely because fewer founders reach them through systematic outreach. They're less crowded inboxes.

## Step 5: Organise Into a Prioritised, Actionable Outreach List

A spreadsheet full of validated investor names is not yet an outreach plan. The final step is translating your list into a ranked, sequenced pipeline that tells you who to contact first, how to reach them, and at what pace.

Score each investor on three dimensions: stage alignment (does your round size match their typical check?), sector fit (does their portfolio history show genuine conviction in your category?), and location alignment (are they actively investing in your city or have they backed companies there before?). A simple 1-to-3 score on each dimension gives you a composite fit score that naturally surfaces the top tier. Move those names to the front of the queue.

For each investor, note the warmest available intro path. 
Investors respond to warm introductions at triple the rate of cold outreach — use the database first to find the connection path, not just the contact email.
 A second-degree LinkedIn connection, a shared accelerator alum, a portfolio founder you can ask for an introduction — these paths exist far more often than founders realise, and they're worth a few hours of network mapping before the first message goes out.

On pace: 
aim to reach 20–30 targeted investors per month rather than blasting thousands of generic emails.
 That cadence is sustainable, allows for genuine personalisation, and generates enough pipeline signal — reply rates, objection patterns, requests for more information — to refine your messaging in real time. Volume without quality doesn't compound; quality at a steady pace does.

## From List to Executed Outreach

Building a location-filtered, validated, prioritised investor list is the research half of fundraising. It is hard, time-consuming, and genuinely important. But it is also only the first half.

The second half — writing personalised first-contact messages that reflect actual research into each investor's thesis, portfolio, and recent activity; following up consistently without becoming noise; managing the thread of ten, twenty, or thirty simultaneous conversations — is where most founder-led outreach stalls. Not because founders lack the skill, but because sustaining that level of research depth across a full list, while also running the company, is genuinely unsustainable without dedicated resource.
